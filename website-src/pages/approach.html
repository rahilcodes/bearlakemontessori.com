Where it began

A doctor who watched children closely.

Dr. Maria Montessori, the creator of what is called “The Montessori Method of Education”, based this new education on her scientific observations of young children’s behavior.

She found that young children learn best in a homelike setting, filled with developmentally appropriate materials that grow self-motivated, independent learners. More than a century later, that is still the blueprint for our classrooms.

  1. 1870

    Born in Italy

    Maria Montessori is born in Chiaravalle, Italy. She goes on to become one of the first women in Italy to qualify as a physician.

  2. 1907

    The Children’s House

    She is invited to open a childcare center for children in Rome. She calls it “The Children’s House” and builds the program around her observations of how young children learn.

  3. 1912

    The method travels

    The Montessori Method is published in English, and schools following her approach begin to open around the world.

  4. Today

    Bear Lake, Altamonte Springs

    Our methodology to teaching is based on the principles of Dr Maria Montessori, who encouraged children to learn naturally through activity.

Ten core principles

The heart of the Montessori approach.

The core principles of Dr. Maria Montessori’s educational approach include:

  1. 01

    Child-led learning

    Children choose activities based on their interests and developmental needs.

  2. 02

    Hands-on exploration

    Learning occurs through purposeful activities and real-world experiences.

  3. 03

    Prepared environment

    Classrooms are carefully designed to encourage independence, order, and discovery.

  4. 04

    Respect for the child

    Teachers recognize each child as capable, unique, and deserving of dignity.

  5. 05

    Self-paced development

    Children learn at their own pace without unnecessary pressure or comparison.

  6. 06

    The teacher as a guide

    Teachers observe, support, and introduce materials rather than directing every activity.

  7. 07

    Mixed-age classrooms

    Younger and older children learn together and support one another.

  8. 08

    Learning through the senses

    Montessori materials help children understand abstract concepts through sensory experiences.

  9. 09

    Independence and responsibility

    Children are encouraged to care for themselves, others, and their environment.

  10. 10

    Holistic development

    The approach supports academic, social, emotional, physical, and practical-life skills.

Spot the difference

How to recognise a Montessori classroom.

A Montessori classroom can be identified by four things. Hover or tap the numbers to find them in one of ours.

  1. 1

    Montessori-educated teachers. Teachers educated in the Montessori philosophy, with the ability and dedication to put the key concepts into practice.

  2. 2

    Mixed ages together. A multi-aged, multi-graded heterogeneous grouping of students, so younger children learn by watching and older children learn by helping.

  3. 3

    Montessori materials. A diverse set of Montessori materials, activities and experiences designed to foster physical, intellectual, creative and social independence.

  4. 4

    A social atmosphere. A classroom atmosphere which encourages social interaction for cooperative learning, peer teaching and emotional development.

The teacher’s role

Show the way, then step back.

In a Montessori classroom the teacher is a guide. The aim of every lesson is a child who no longer needs the teacher for that task.

  1. 1

    The child chooses

    Children make independent choices in the areas that are of greatest interest to them.

  2. 2

    The teacher shows

    The teacher presents the activity and shows how to follow the choice through, step by step.

  3. 3

    The child practises

    The child repeats the work as often as they like, building concentration and confidence.

  4. 4

    The teacher withdraws

    Once the activity is mastered, the teacher can withdraw and the child is able to work independently.

Different by design

What makes Montessori different?

Parents often ask how a Montessori day compares with a conventional preschool. These are the differences you will notice first.

In our Montessori classrooms

  • Children choose their workWithin a prepared environment, each child picks the activity that interests them most.
  • Mixed ages learn togetherYounger children watch and copy; older children consolidate what they know by helping.
  • Hands come firstConcrete materials give a physical impression of an idea before it becomes abstract.
  • The teacher guidesLessons are given one-to-one or in small groups, then the teacher steps back.
  • Each child sets the paceLearning is individualized, with daily group projects for social learning.

In many traditional classrooms

  • The schedule sets the activityThe whole class usually moves from one task to the next at the same time.
  • Children are grouped by ageEach class tends to contain children of a single age.
  • Worksheets and screensIdeas are often introduced on paper first, rather than through the hands.
  • The teacher leads from the frontLessons are typically given to the whole group at once.
  • One pace for everyoneChildren work through the same material on the same timeline.
